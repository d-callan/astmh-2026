The Problem: SRA is Vast but Unqueryable

One of BRC Analytics' aims is to support global pathogen surveillance — so we partnered with the Logan project.

  • SRA holds petabytes of pathogen sequencing — clinical isolates, metagenomes, wastewater, wildlife.
  • Almost all of it is effectively invisible: searchable by metadata — accession, project title — but not by sequence content.
The question nobody could ask

"Show me every sample containing this gene" — there was no way to ask it without downloading petabytes.

Motivation
Open with the mission line: global surveillance is one of our aims, Logan is the partner — then land the problem. Make it concrete for field researchers: you suspect a marker gene is out there — a resistance determinant, a vaccine target. Metadata search can only find what the submitter bothered to label. Three Tools, Three Levels of Specificity

kmindex
k-mer lookup — "is this sequence in the archive?" Near-exact: primers, markers.
~ minutes
LexicMap
Alignment — "anything like this sequence?" Tolerates SNPs/indels, BLAST-like scores.
up to hours
Protein search
"Anything encoding something like this protein?" — the most divergent homologs.
up to hours
One input, three depths

Same question — "which samples contain this?" — asked at increasing tolerance for divergence. All three run on Galaxy today; the workflows packaging them for gene pages are what's still coming.

Search tools
The ladder: k-mers = strictest and fastest; alignment = tolerant; protein = catches what nucleotide-level search misses entirely. Expectation-setting on runtime: deeper search, longer wait — results land in your history when done.

What a Search Returns

  • Whatever the tool, the answer has the same shape: a ranked list of hits — which SRA samples contain your sequence — with match statistics and confidence metrics.
  • The uniform point: the archive is queryable by content — annotation doesn't matter.
  • One marker gene → a cohort of accessions in minutes to hours — a question that used to mean weeks of scripting and downloading.
What next?

Fetching the underlying reads and reconstructing haplotypes from the hits — that's where the in-development workflows come in.

Results
Deliberately generic on output formats — every tool describes hits in its own way; the audience doesn't care. Honest framing: tools give you the hit list today; the fetch/haplotype steps are workflows still in development.

Next Step: Haplotypes from Hits in development

  • disassembler / logan-walker — our tool for closing the loop. For each hit accession it streams that run's unitig graph straight from Logan's public S3 bucket, anchors your gene in it, and walks the graph to reconstruct the complete coding haplotype — including within-sample variant alleles.
  • A faster route works from LexicMap hits against Logan contigs — tiled onto the reference into a codon-checked cohort.

On bioconda today; Galaxy wrappers under review — a prototype, not yet on Galaxy or BRC Analytics. One of several Logan use-case workflows destined for the in-development gene pages.

disassembler
Mechanism in one breath: hit accession → stream its unitig graph from S3 → anchor the query gene by shared k-mers → walk with GraphAligner → full-length haplotype, including intra-host variants. GitHub: nekrut/disassembler — QR on the resources slide.
